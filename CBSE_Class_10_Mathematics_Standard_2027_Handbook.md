## Chapter 1: Real Numbers

### A. Chapter overview
Prime factorisation reveals the structure of whole numbers. Use it to compare divisibility, find HCF and LCM, and prove that certain square roots cannot be rational.

**Objectives:** apply the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic; find HCF/LCM; construct a contradiction proof of irrationality.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
- A prime has exactly two positive factors, 1 and itself. Every composite integer greater than 1 is a product of primes.
- **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic:** this prime factorisation is unique apart from the order of factors.
- For positive integers, HCF uses the smallest exponents common to both prime factorizations; LCM uses the largest exponents appearing.
- To prove an irrationality statement, assume the number is a reduced fraction, then show its numerator and denominator share a prime factor, contradicting reduction.

#### Visual guide: prime factor tree

```text
             60
           /    \
          6      10
         / \     / \
        2   3   2   5
```

The leaves are prime, so $60=2^2\cdot3\cdot5$.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Formulae and number facts

| Name | Formula / fact | Meaning and conditions | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prime factorisation | $n=p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_k^{a_k}$ | Distinct primes $p_i$, positive integer exponents $a_i$, and $n>1$ | Express an integer as prime powers |
| HCF–LCM relation | $\operatorname{HCF}(a,b)\operatorname{LCM}(a,b)=ab$ | Positive integers $a,b$ | Check or find one of HCF and LCM when the other is known |

#### Theorem
**Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic:** Every integer greater than 1 is either prime or can be expressed as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique apart from the order of the factors.

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: HCF and LCM**

**Question:** Find the HCF and LCM of 72 and 120.

**Concept used:** Prime exponents.

**Solution:**
1. $72=2^3\cdot3^2$ and $120=2^3\cdot3\cdot5$.
2. HCF $=2^3\cdot3=24$; LCM $=2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5=360$.
3. Check: $24\cdot360=72\cdot120$.

**Final answer:** **HCF = 24, LCM = 360.**

**Exam tip:** Include each prime in the LCM using its greatest exponent.

**Example 2: A meeting interval**

**Question:** Two bells ring every 18 and 24 minutes. If they ring together now, after how many minutes will they next ring together?

**Concept used:** LCM.

**Solution:**
1. $18=2\cdot3^2$; $24=2^3\cdot3$.
2. $\operatorname{LCM}=2^3\cdot3^2=72$.

**Final answer:** **72 minutes.**

**Exam tip:** Repeated events coincide after a common multiple; the first repeat is the LCM.

**Example 3: Prove $\sqrt5$ is irrational**

**Question:** Prove that $\sqrt5$ is irrational.

**Concept used:** Contradiction and prime divisibility.

**Solution:**
1. Suppose $\sqrt5=a/b$ in lowest terms, with integers $a,b$ and $b\ne0$.
2. Squaring gives $a^2=5b^2$, so 5 divides $a^2$ and hence 5 divides $a$.
3. Write $a=5k$. Then $25k^2=5b^2$, so $b^2=5k^2$ and 5 divides $b$.
4. This contradicts that $a/b$ is in lowest terms.

**Final answer:** **$\sqrt5$ is irrational.**

**Exam tip:** State why divisibility of a prime into a square implies divisibility into its root.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Using smallest exponents for LCM; use the greatest.
- Forgetting 1 is not prime; it has only one positive factor.
- Calling an un-reduced fraction a contradiction; specify lowest terms.
- Writing only the conclusion in an irrationality proof; show the divisibility contradiction.
- Applying the HCF–LCM product formula without positive integers.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Write prime factorizations in aligned form. For a proof, explicitly assume a reduced fraction and justify every divisibility step. For word problems, decide whether the situation asks for the largest equal grouping (HCF) or the first shared repeat (LCM).

### G. Quick revision summary
Use unique prime factorisation; HCF takes common primes with minimum powers, LCM takes all primes with maximum powers. Irrationality proofs use a lowest-terms assumption and contradiction.

### H. Chapter-end revision checklist
- [ ] I can prime-factorise integers and identify primes.
- [ ] I can find HCF and LCM and check their product.
- [ ] I can distinguish grouping (HCF) from repeating (LCM).
- [ ] I can write an irrationality proof clearly.

### I. Practice questions
1. Find the HCF and LCM of 84 and 126.
2. Three lights flash every 12, 15, and 20 seconds. When do they next flash together?
3. Prove that $\sqrt2$ is irrational.
4. Explain why $3+2\sqrt5$ is irrational.

**Answers / hints:** 1. 42 and 252. 2. 60 seconds. 3. Assume $\sqrt2=a/b$ reduced; $a^2=2b^2$ forces both even. 4. If the expression were rational, subtract 3 and divide by 2 to make $\sqrt5$ rational.

## Chapter 2: Polynomials

### A. Chapter overview
Polynomial zeros are the inputs where its value is zero. For a quadratic, they also encode the coefficients.

**Objectives:** identify zeros from graphs or algebra; use and verify the zero-coefficient relationships; construct a quadratic from given zeros.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
- A zero of $p(x)$ is a value $r$ for which $p(r)=0$. Graphically it is an x-coordinate where the curve meets or touches the x-axis.
- For $ax^2+bx+c$, with zeros $\alpha,\beta$, $\alpha+\beta=-b/a$ and $\alpha\beta=c/a$.
- The monic quadratic with roots $\alpha,\beta$ is $(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)$.

#### Visual guide: zeros are x-intercepts

```text
       y
       ↑       \       /
       |        \_____/
───────●───────────────●──────→ x
       α               β
```

The curve meets the x-axis at $\alpha$ and $\beta$; those x-coordinates are its zeros.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Formulae

| Name | Formula | Meaning and conditions | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sum of zeros | $\alpha+\beta=-b/a$ | For $ax^2+bx+c$, $a\ne0$ | Check or find the sum of the two zeros |
| Product of zeros | $\alpha\beta=c/a$ | For $ax^2+bx+c$, $a\ne0$ | Check or find the product of the two zeros |
| Monic polynomial from zeros | $x^2-(\alpha+\beta)x+\alpha\beta$ | Zeros are $\alpha,\beta$ | Construct a monic quadratic |

#### Geometrical property
A real zero is the $x$-coordinate at which the graph of $y=p(x)$ intersects or touches the $x$-axis.

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: Find zeros**

**Question:** Find the zeros of $x^2-5x+6$ and verify the relationships.

**Concept used:** Factorisation and coefficient relations.

**Solution:**
1. $x^2-5x+6=(x-2)(x-3)$.
2. Zeros are 2 and 3. Their sum is 5, while $-b/a=-(-5)/1=5$.
3. Their product is 6, while $c/a=6/1=6$.

**Final answer:** **Zeros: 2 and 3; both relationships hold.**

**Exam tip:** Match the polynomial to $ax^2+bx+c$ before substituting.

**Example 2: Form a polynomial**

**Question:** Form a monic quadratic polynomial whose zeros are $-2$ and 5.

**Concept used:** Product of root factors.

**Solution:**
1. $p(x)=(x+2)(x-5)$.
2. Expanding gives $x^2-3x-10$.

**Final answer:** **$x^2-3x-10$.**

**Exam tip:** Verify: sum $=3=-(-3)$ and product $=-10$.

**Example 3: Graph interpretation**

**Question:** A quadratic graph crosses the x-axis at $x=-1$ and $x=4$. State its zeros and the sum and product.

**Concept used:** Geometrical interpretation and root relationships.

**Solution:**
1. The x-intercepts give zeros $-1$ and 4.
2. Sum $=-1+4=3$; product $=(-1)(4)=-4$.

**Final answer:** **Zeros $-1,4$; sum 3; product $-4$.**

**Exam tip:** A repeated touch at the axis counts as a zero too.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Confusing x-intercepts with y-intercepts.
- Using $b/a$ instead of $-b/a$ for the sum.
- Forgetting the leading coefficient when applying the relations.
- Reporting factors' signs as roots without solving each factor.
- Forming a polynomial without expanding or stating whether it is monic.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Use factorisation for simple integer roots; use relationships to check work. For a graph, read the horizontal coordinates at the axis crossings. A polynomial may have fewer real zeros than its degree.

### G. Quick revision summary
Zeros satisfy $p(r)=0$. For a quadratic, sum $=-b/a$, product $=c/a$. The monic polynomial from two zeros is $(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)$.

### H. Chapter-end revision checklist
- [ ] I can find zeros by factorisation.
- [ ] I can read zeros from a graph.
- [ ] I can verify sum and product relationships.
- [ ] I can construct a quadratic from its zeros.

### I. Practice questions
1. Find and verify the zeros of $2x^2-7x+3$.
2. Form a monic quadratic with zeros 3 and 7.
3. A graph meets the x-axis at 2 only, touching it. What is its zero and what may its multiplicity be?

**Answers / hints:** 1. $3,\frac12$; sum $7/2$, product $3/2$. 2. $x^2-10x+21$. 3. Zero 2; it may be a repeated zero.

## Chapter 3: Pair of Linear Equations in Two Variables

### A. Chapter overview
A pair of linear equations represents two lines. Their intersection, coincidence, or separation describes the number of solutions.

**Objectives:** classify pairs; solve graphically, by substitution, and by elimination; model simple situations.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
For $a_1x+b_1y+c_1=0$ and $a_2x+b_2y+c_2=0$:
- Unique solution if $\frac{a_1}{a_2}\ne\frac{b_1}{b_2}$ (intersecting lines).
- No solution if $\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac{b_1}{b_2}\ne\frac{c_1}{c_2}$ (parallel distinct lines).
- Infinitely many if all three ratios are equal (coincident lines).
Substitution isolates one variable; elimination combines equations to remove a variable. Graphically, a solution is a common point.

#### Visual guide: one solution is an intersection

```text
        y
        ↑  \ /
        |   X  (common point)
        |  / \
        +────────→ x
```

Two non-parallel lines meet at one point. Parallel distinct lines do not meet; coincident lines share every point.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Consistency test

| Pair of lines | Coefficient condition | Number of solutions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intersecting | $\frac{a_1}{a_2}\ne\frac{b_1}{b_2}$ | One |
| Parallel and distinct | $\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac{b_1}{b_2}\ne\frac{c_1}{c_2}$ | None |
| Coincident | $\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac{b_1}{b_2}=\frac{c_1}{c_2}$ | Infinitely many |

These ratio forms assume the denominators are non-zero; when they are not, compare coefficients by cross-multiplication.

#### Solution methods

| Method | Main step | Best used when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substitution | Isolate one variable and substitute into the other equation | A variable is easy to isolate |
| Elimination | Add or subtract suitable multiples to remove a variable | Coefficients can be made equal or opposite |
| Graphical | Plot both lines and locate their common point | The question asks for a visual interpretation |

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: Elimination**

**Question:** Solve $2x+y=7$, $x-y=2$.

**Concept used:** Elimination.

**Solution:**
1. Add the equations: $3x=9$, so $x=3$.
2. Substitute in $x-y=2$: $3-y=2$, hence $y=1$.
3. Check: $2(3)+1=7$.

**Final answer:** **$(x,y)=(3,1)$.**

**Exam tip:** Substitute the result into both original equations.

**Example 2: Classify a pair**

**Question:** Determine the number of solutions of $2x+4y=6$ and $x+2y=5$.

**Concept used:** Ratio condition.

**Solution:**
1. Write in standard form: $2x+4y-6=0$, $x+2y-5=0$.
2. Ratios are $a_1/a_2=2$, $b_1/b_2=2$, and $c_1/c_2=6/5$.
3. The first two match but the third differs, so lines are parallel and distinct.

**Final answer:** **No solution; the pair is inconsistent.**

**Exam tip:** Preserve signs when writing the constant terms.

**Example 3: Word problem**

**Question:** Two adult tickets and one child ticket cost ₹110; one adult and two child tickets cost ₹100. Find each price.

**Concept used:** Modelling and elimination.

**Solution:**
1. Let adult price be $a$ and child price be $c$. Then $2a+c=110$, $a+2c=100$.
2. Double the second equation: $2a+4c=200$. Subtract the first: $3c=90$, so $c=30$.
3. Then $2a+30=110$, so $a=40$.

**Final answer:** **Adult ₹40; child ₹30.**

**Exam tip:** Define variables with units before forming equations.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Dividing ratios when a denominator is zero; compare by cross-products if needed.
- Incorrect signs in elimination.
- Treating a parallel pair as coincident.
- Giving only one coordinate rather than an ordered pair.
- Forming equations that do not match the wording.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Choose a method that keeps arithmetic simple. For graphical solutions, label axes and plot both lines accurately. In word problems, define variables, write two independent equations, solve, then check in context.

### G. Quick revision summary
Intersecting lines give one solution; coincident lines infinitely many; distinct parallel lines none. Verify an algebraic solution in both equations.

### H. Chapter-end revision checklist
- [ ] I can classify a pair using coefficient ratios.
- [ ] I can solve using substitution and elimination.
- [ ] I can explain a graphical solution.
- [ ] I can translate a simple situation into two equations.

### I. Practice questions
1. Solve $3x+2y=12$, $x-y=1$.
2. Classify $x+2y=3$ and $2x+4y=8$.
3. The sum of two numbers is 31 and their difference is 7. Find them.

**Answers / hints:** 1. $x=2,y=3$. 2. No solution. 3. 19 and 12.

## Chapter 4: Quadratic Equations

### A. Chapter overview
A quadratic equation has degree two. Factorisation or the quadratic formula finds its roots; the discriminant predicts their nature.

**Objectives:** solve by prescribed methods; interpret the discriminant; model simple area or number situations.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
Standard form is $ax^2+bx+c=0$, $a\ne0$. Roots are values satisfying the equation. For discriminant $D=b^2-4ac$: $D>0$ gives two distinct real roots; $D=0$ gives equal real roots; $D<0$ gives no real roots.

#### Visual guide: roots and the x-axis

```text
       y
       ↑       \       /
       |        \_____/
───────●───────────────●──────→ x
    root 1           root 2
```

For this downward-opening sketch, the two x-intercepts represent two distinct real roots. A graph may instead touch the axis once or miss it.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Formulae

| Name | Formula | Meaning and conditions | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quadratic formula | $x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$ | $ax^2+bx+c=0$, $a\ne0$ | Find roots when factorisation is inconvenient |
| Discriminant | $D=b^2-4ac$ | $a,b,c$ are coefficients in standard form | Determine the nature of real roots |

#### Root classification

| Discriminant | Nature of roots |
| --- | --- |
| $D>0$ | Two distinct real roots |
| $D=0$ | Two equal real roots |
| $D<0$ | No real roots |

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: Factorisation**

**Question:** Solve $x^2-7x+12=0$.

**Concept used:** Factorisation.

**Solution:**
1. Find factors of 12 whose sum is 7: 3 and 4.
2. $(x-3)(x-4)=0$.
3. Thus $x=3$ or $x=4$.

**Final answer:** **$x=3,4$.**

**Exam tip:** Check each root in the original equation.

**Example 2: Quadratic formula**

**Question:** Solve $2x^2+x-3=0$.

**Concept used:** Quadratic formula.

**Solution:**
1. $a=2,b=1,c=-3$; $D=1+24=25$.
2. $x=\frac{-1\pm5}{4}$.
3. Hence $x=1$ or $x=-3/2$.

**Final answer:** **$1,-\frac32$.**

**Exam tip:** Keep the denominator $2a$ outside the complete numerator.

**Example 3: Nature of roots**

**Question:** Find $k$ if $x^2+4x+k=0$ has equal roots.

**Concept used:** Equal-root condition.

**Solution:**
1. Equal roots require $D=0$.
2. $4^2-4(1)(k)=0$, so $16-4k=0$.
3. Thus $k=4$.

**Final answer:** **$k=4$.**

**Exam tip:** Use the discriminant condition before attempting to solve.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Failing to put the equation in standard form before identifying coefficients.
- Losing the negative sign in $-b$ or in $c$.
- Using $\sqrt{b^2}-4ac$ instead of $\sqrt{b^2-4ac}$.
- Misclassifying $D=0$ as two distinct roots.
- Keeping extraneous roots without checking a contextual restriction.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Factor when integer factors are apparent; otherwise use the formula. Write $a,b,c$ explicitly. For a word problem, reject values that contradict the described quantity, such as a negative length.

### G. Quick revision summary
Standard form requires $a\ne0$. Discriminant $D=b^2-4ac$ classifies real roots. The quadratic formula works for all real-coefficient quadratics with real roots as applicable.

### H. Chapter-end revision checklist
- [ ] I can solve by factorisation.
- [ ] I can substitute coefficients accurately into the formula.
- [ ] I can classify roots from the discriminant.
- [ ] I can check contextual answers.

### I. Practice questions
1. Solve $x^2-9x+20=0$.
2. Solve $3x^2-2x-1=0$.
3. For what $p$ does $x^2+px+9=0$ have equal roots?

**Answers / hints:** 1. 4, 5. 2. 1, $-1/3$. 3. $p=\pm6$.

## Chapter 5: Arithmetic Progressions

### A. Chapter overview
An arithmetic progression (AP) adds the same common difference at each step. Its term and sum formulas follow from this regular pattern.

**Objectives:** identify an AP; find terms, number of terms, and sums; model repeated increases or decreases.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
An AP has terms $a,a+d,a+2d,\ldots$, where $a$ is first term and $d$ common difference. Subtract consecutive terms to test whether the difference is constant.

#### Visual guide: equal steps

```text
 a        a+d       a+2d      a+3d
 ●─────────●─────────●─────────●────→
      d         d         d
```

Every step adds the same common difference $d$; $d$ can also be negative.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Formulae

| Name | Formula | Meaning and conditions | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $n$th term | $a_n=a+(n-1)d$ | $a$ first term; $d$ common difference; $n\ge1$ | Find a specified term or term number |
| Sum of first $n$ terms | $S_n=\frac n2[2a+(n-1)d]$ | $a$ first term; $d$ common difference; $n\ge1$ | Find a total without adding each term |
| Sum using last term | $S_n=\frac n2(a+l)$ | $l$ is the last included term | Use when the first and last terms are known |

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: Find a term**

**Question:** Find the 20th term of $5,8,11,\ldots$.

**Concept used:** $n$th-term formula.

**Solution:**
1. $a=5,d=3,n=20$.
2. $a_{20}=5+19(3)=62$.

**Final answer:** **62.**

**Exam tip:** The multiplier is $n-1$, not $n$.

**Example 2: Sum**

**Question:** Find the sum of the first 15 terms of $2,5,8,\ldots$.

**Concept used:** AP sum.

**Solution:**
1. $a=2,d=3,n=15$.
2. $S_{15}=\frac{15}{2}[4+14(3)]=\frac{15}{2}(46)=345$.

**Final answer:** **345.**

**Exam tip:** Check whether the expression inside brackets is even before simplifying.

**Example 3: Saving pattern**

**Question:** A student saves ₹10 in week 1 and increases the saving by ₹5 each week. How much is saved in week 12?

**Concept used:** $n$th term.

**Solution:**
1. $a=10,d=5,n=12$.
2. $a_{12}=10+11(5)=65$.

**Final answer:** **₹65 in week 12.**

**Exam tip:** A question asking “in week 12” asks for a term, not the total.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Using the wrong first term after skipping terms.
- Reversing the subtraction order for $d$ midway.
- Confusing a term with the sum of terms.
- Substituting $n$ instead of $n-1$.
- Forgetting units or rupees in application questions.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Write $a,d,n$ first. For a missing term or term count, set the formula equal to the given value and solve. If the pattern decreases, $d$ is negative.

### G. Quick revision summary
Constant consecutive difference defines an AP. Use $a+(n-1)d$ for one term and the sum formula for a cumulative total.

### H. Chapter-end revision checklist
- [ ] I can identify $a$ and $d$.
- [ ] I can find a specified term or term number.
- [ ] I can calculate a partial sum.
- [ ] I can distinguish a single term from a cumulative quantity.

### I. Practice questions
1. Find the 25th term of $7,3,-1,\ldots$.
2. Find the sum of the first 10 terms of $4,7,10,\ldots$.
3. Which term of $2,6,10,\ldots$ is 50?

**Answers / hints:** 1. $-89$. 2. 175. 3. 13th.

## Chapter 6: Triangles

### A. Chapter overview
Similar triangles have the same shape: corresponding angles are equal and corresponding sides are proportional. Proportionality links geometry to measurement.

**Objectives:** apply similarity criteria; prove and use BPT and its converse; compare areas of similar triangles.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
- AA similarity: two corresponding angles equal. SSS: corresponding sides proportional. SAS: included angle equal and surrounding sides proportional.
- **BPT:** A line parallel to one side of a triangle divides the other two sides in the same ratio.
- Proof outline: for $\triangle ABC$, if $D$ lies on $AB$, $E$ on $AC$, and $DE\parallel BC$, compare areas of triangles sharing the same altitude; use equal areas for bases on the same line and equal areas for triangles on the same base and between parallels to obtain $AD/DB=AE/EC$.
- Converse: if $AD/DB=AE/EC$, then $DE\parallel BC$.
- Similar triangles have area ratio equal to the square of the corresponding-side ratio.

#### Visual guide: Basic Proportionality Theorem

```text
             A
            / \
           D---E       DE ∥ BC
          /     \
         B-------C
```

With $D$ on $AB$ and $E$ on $AC$, the parallel segment creates corresponding side ratios.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Formulae

| Name | Relationship | Meaning and conditions | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| BPT ratio | $\frac{AD}{DB}=\frac{AE}{EC}$ | In $\triangle ABC$, $D$ lies on $AB$, $E$ on $AC$, and $DE\parallel BC$ | Find a missing side segment |
| Similar-triangle area ratio | $\frac{[\triangle_1]}{[\triangle_2]}=(\frac{\text{corresponding side}_1}{\text{corresponding side}_2})^2$ | Triangles are similar and sides are in matching order | Compare areas from a side ratio |

#### Theorems and similarity criteria

| Result | Statement | Proof / use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic Proportionality Theorem | A line parallel to one side of a triangle divides the other two sides in the same ratio | Proof required; use when a parallel segment is given |
| Converse of BPT | If a line divides two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, it is parallel to the third side | Apply to establish parallelism |
| AA similarity | Two corresponding angles are equal | State and apply as a similarity criterion |
| SSS similarity | Corresponding sides are proportional | State and apply as a similarity criterion |
| SAS similarity | Two pairs of corresponding sides are proportional and their included angles are equal | State and apply as a similarity criterion |

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: BPT calculation**

**Question:** In $\triangle ABC$, $DE\parallel BC$, $AD=3$, $DB=2$, and $AE=6$. Find $EC$.

**Concept used:** BPT.

**Solution:**
1. $AD/DB=AE/EC$.
2. $3/2=6/EC$, so $3EC=12$.
3. $EC=4$.

**Final answer:** **$EC=4$.**

**Exam tip:** Match the segments in the same order along each side.

**Example 2: Similarity by AA**

**Question:** Two triangles each have angles $40^\circ$ and $70^\circ$. Are they similar?

**Concept used:** AA criterion.

**Solution:**
1. The third angle in each is $180^\circ-40^\circ-70^\circ=70^\circ$.
2. Two corresponding angles are equal, so the triangles are similar by AA.

**Final answer:** **Yes, they are similar by AA.**

**Exam tip:** Two angles suffice because the third follows from the angle sum.

**Example 3: Area ratio**

**Question:** Similar triangles have corresponding side ratio $3:5$. Find their area ratio.

**Concept used:** Squares of corresponding side ratio.

**Solution:**
1. Area ratio $=3^2:5^2$.
2. Therefore it is $9:25$.

**Final answer:** **$9:25$.**

**Exam tip:** Do not use the side ratio directly as the area ratio.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Matching non-corresponding vertices or sides.
- Using BPT without the parallel condition.
- Reversing a ratio on only one side.
- Claiming similarity from equal sides without checking correspondence.
- Forgetting to square side ratios for areas.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Mark equal angles and parallel lines on a diagram. In proofs, name the criterion and state the correspondence order. In BPT calculations, establish the proportionality before substituting lengths.

### G. Quick revision summary
AA, SSS, and SAS establish similarity. BPT applies when a line is parallel; its converse tests parallelism. Similar-triangle area ratios are squares of side ratios.

- [ ] I can choose AA, SSS, or SAS correctly.
- [ ] I can identify corresponding sides.

**Answers / hints:** 1. $7.5$. 2. $4:49$. 3. SAS.

## Chapter 7: Coordinate Geometry

### A. Chapter overview
Coordinate formulas turn geometric questions into calculations with ordered pairs.

**Objectives:** calculate distances; find an internally dividing point; use the midpoint as a special case.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
The distance formula follows from Pythagoras: horizontal change is $x_2-x_1$, vertical change is $y_2-y_1$. Section formula uses a weighted average, with the nearer endpoint receiving the larger weight.

#### Visual guide: internal division

```text
             P divides AB in m:n
      A────────────P────────────B
             AP:PB = m:n
```

The section formula weights the coordinates of $B$ by $m$ and those of $A$ by $n$.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Formulae

| Name | Formula | Meaning and conditions | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Distance | $d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}$ | Points $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$ | Find the straight-line distance |
| Internal section | $(\frac{mx_2+nx_1}{m+n},\frac{my_2+ny_1}{m+n})$ | $P$ divides $A(x_1,y_1)B(x_2,y_2)$ internally in ratio $m:n$, $m,n>0$ | Find a point between two endpoints |
| Midpoint | $(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\frac{y_1+y_2}{2})$ | Special case of ratio $1:1$ | Find the halfway point |

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: Distance**

**Question:** Find the distance between $(1,2)$ and $(4,6)$.

**Concept used:** Distance formula.

**Solution:**
1. $d=\sqrt{(4-1)^2+(6-2)^2}$.
2. $d=\sqrt{9+16}=5$.

**Final answer:** **5 units.**

**Exam tip:** Square the signed differences; their signs do not affect the result.

**Example 2: Internal division**

**Question:** Find the point dividing $A(1,2)$ and $B(7,8)$ in ratio $1:2$.

**Concept used:** Section formula.

**Solution:**
1. $m=1,n=2$.
2. Coordinates are $(\frac{1(7)+2(1)}3,\frac{1(8)+2(2)}3)$.
3. The point is $(3,4)$.

**Final answer:** **$(3,4)$.**

**Exam tip:** State clearly which endpoint is associated with $m$.

**Example 3: Midpoint**

**Question:** Find the midpoint of $(-2,5)$ and $(6,1)$.

**Concept used:** Midpoint formula.

**Solution:**
1. Average the x-coordinates: $(-2+6)/2=2$.
2. Average the y-coordinates: $(5+1)/2=3$.

**Final answer:** **$(2,3)$.**

**Exam tip:** Average coordinates separately.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Omitting a square before adding coordinate differences.
- Mixing x- and y-coordinates.
- Reversing the section-ratio weights.
- Dividing only one coordinate by $m+n$.
- Confusing distance with squared distance.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Write the formula before substituting. For a section point, test whether the result lies between the endpoints. Keep exact radicals unless a decimal is requested.

### G. Quick revision summary
Distance is the Pythagorean length. Internal section coordinates are weighted averages; midpoint is the equal-weight case.

### H. Chapter-end revision checklist
- [ ] I can calculate distance between any two points.
- [ ] I can use the internal section formula with correct weights.
- [ ] I can find and interpret a midpoint.

### I. Practice questions
1. Find the distance between $(0,-1)$ and $(6,7)$.
2. Find the midpoint of $(3,4)$ and $(9,-2)$.
3. Find the point dividing $(0,0)$ and $(8,4)$ in ratio $3:1$.

**Answers / hints:** 1. 10. 2. $(6,1)$. 3. $(6,3)$.

## Chapter 8: Introduction to Trigonometry

### A. Chapter overview
Trigonometric ratios compare sides of a right triangle relative to an acute angle.

**Objectives:** define six ratios; recall standard values; use reciprocal relationships and the fundamental identity.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
Relative to angle $A$, opposite, adjacent, and hypotenuse identify sides. $\sin A=O/H$, $\cos A=A/H$, $\tan A=O/A$; cosec, sec, and cot are reciprocals. Ratios depend on the angle, not the triangle's size.

#### Visual guide: name sides from angle $A$

```text
                 C
                 |\
  opposite (O)   | \  hypotenuse (H)
                 |  \
                 |___\
                 B   A
                  adjacent (Adj)
```

The right angle is at $B$; for angle $A$, the opposite side is $BC$ and the adjacent side is $AB$.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Trigonometric ratios

| Ratio | Definition | Conditions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\sin A$ | $\frac{O}{H}$ | Acute angle in a right triangle |
| $\cos A$ | $\frac{Adj}{H}$ | Acute angle in a right triangle |
| $\tan A$ | $\frac{O}{Adj}$ | Undefined when $Adj=0$ |
| $\cosec A$ | $\frac{H}{O}=\frac1{\sin A}$ | Undefined when $\sin A=0$ |
| $\sec A$ | $\frac{H}{Adj}=\frac1{\cos A}$ | Undefined when $\cos A=0$ |
| $\cot A$ | $\frac{Adj}{O}=\frac1{\tan A}$ | Undefined when $\tan A=0$ |

| Angle | $0^\circ$ | $30^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $90^\circ$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $\sin$ | 0 | $1/2$ | $1/\sqrt2$ | $\sqrt3/2$ | 1 |
| $\cos$ | 1 | $\sqrt3/2$ | $1/\sqrt2$ | $1/2$ | 0 |
| $\tan$ | 0 | $1/\sqrt3$ | 1 | $\sqrt3$ | undefined |

#### Identity

| Name | Identity | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fundamental identity | $\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1$ | Relate sine and cosine or verify a simple identity |

At $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, check the denominator before using any reciprocal ratio.

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: Find all ratios**

**Question:** A right triangle has opposite side 3, adjacent side 4, and hypotenuse 5 relative to $A$. Find its six ratios.

**Concept used:** Definitions.

**Solution:**
1. $\sin A=3/5$, $\cos A=4/5$, $\tan A=3/4$.
2. Reciprocals give $\cosec A=5/3$, $\sec A=5/4$, $\cot A=4/3$.

**Final answer:** **$3/5,4/5,3/4,5/3,5/4,4/3$ respectively.**

**Exam tip:** Identify the angle before labelling opposite and adjacent.

**Example 2: Identity**

**Question:** If $\sin A=3/5$ and $A$ is acute, find $\cos A$.

**Concept used:** Fundamental identity.

**Solution:**
1. $\cos^2A=1-\sin^2A=1-9/25=16/25$.
2. Since $A$ is acute, $\cos A>0$, so $\cos A=4/5$.

**Final answer:** **$4/5$.**

**Exam tip:** Use the acute-angle condition to choose the positive root.

**Example 3: Standard values**

**Question:** Evaluate $\sin30^\circ+\cos60^\circ+\tan45^\circ$.

**Concept used:** Standard values.

**Solution:**
1. Substitute: $1/2+1/2+1=2$.

**Final answer:** **2.**

**Exam tip:** Memorise the value table and check definedness before using a ratio.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Swapping opposite and adjacent for a different marked angle.
- Treating cosec as $1-\sin$ instead of $1/\sin$.
- Forgetting $\tan90^\circ$ is undefined.
- Dropping the positive sign condition for an acute angle.
- Squaring only one term in an identity.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Sketch and label the right triangle. Reduce ratios, use reciprocals carefully, and state undefined cases rather than assigning a numerical value. Verify simple identities by substituting known ratios.

### G. Quick revision summary
SOH-CAH-TOA defines the three primary ratios. Their reciprocals are cosec, sec, cot. For acute angles, ratios are positive; $\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1$.

- [ ] I can apply the fundamental identity.

2. Evaluate $2\sin30^\circ+\sec60^\circ$.
3. State $\cot90^\circ$.

**Answers / hints:** 1. $5/13,12/13$. 2. 3. 3. 0.

## Chapter 9: Some Applications of Trigonometry

### A. Chapter overview
Angles of elevation and depression connect a line of sight to a right-triangle model for heights and distances.

**Objectives:** draw a labelled diagram; choose a ratio; solve situations involving at most two right triangles.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
- The line of sight joins the observer's eye to the object.
- Elevation is measured upward from the horizontal; depression is measured downward.
- Horizontal lines are parallel, so the angle of depression equals the corresponding angle of elevation.
- The angle is measured from the horizontal, not the vertical.

#### Visual guide: angle of elevation

```text
        T
       /|
      / | h
     /  |
    / θ |
   O────B
     d
```

Here $O$ is the observer and $B$ the tower base. For level ground, $\tan\theta=h/d$. If eye height is included, measure $h$ from the observer's eye level.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Ratios used in height-and-distance models

| Name | Relationship | Conditions | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tangent | $\tan\theta=\frac{\text{vertical height}}{\text{horizontal distance}}$ | Right triangle; $\theta$ measured from horizontal | Find height or horizontal distance |
| Sine | $\sin\theta=\frac{\text{vertical height}}{\text{line of sight}}$ | Right triangle | Find height or line-of-sight length |
| Cosine | $\cos\theta=\frac{\text{horizontal distance}}{\text{line of sight}}$ | Right triangle | Find horizontal distance or line of sight |

#### Angle property
For parallel horizontal lines, the angle of depression from one line equals the corresponding angle of elevation from the other.

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: Height from a distance**

**Question:** From a point 20 m from a tower's base, its angle of elevation is $45^\circ$. Find the tower height, assuming level ground and eye at ground level.

**Concept used:** Tangent.

**Solution:**
1. Let height be $h$. Then $\tan45^\circ=h/20$.
2. Since $\tan45^\circ=1$, $h=20$.

**Final answer:** **20 m.**

**Exam tip:** State the ground-level assumption if eye height is ignored.

**Example 2: Slant distance**

**Question:** A kite string of length 30 m makes a $30^\circ$ angle with the horizontal. Find its vertical height above the holder's hand.

**Concept used:** Sine.

**Solution:**
1. Height is opposite the angle; string is hypotenuse.
2. $\sin30^\circ=h/30$, so $h=15$.

**Final answer:** **15 m above the hand.**

**Exam tip:** The string length is slant distance, not horizontal distance.

**Example 3: Two observation points**

**Question:** A vertical pole is observed from two points on the same side. The nearer point is 10 m from its base and sees the top at $60^\circ$; the farther point is 10 m beyond it. Find the height, using the nearer observation.

**Concept used:** Tangent and model selection.

**Solution:**
1. Let height be $h$. The nearer distance is given as 10 m.
2. $\tan60^\circ=h/10$, so $h=10\sqrt3$ m.

**Final answer:** **$10\sqrt3$ m.**

**Exam tip:** Use the observation with complete known horizontal distance; do not introduce unnecessary equations.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Measuring an angle from the vertical instead of horizontal.
- Confusing slant length and horizontal distance.
- Forgetting the observer's eye height when it is specified.
- Using the wrong ratio for the labelled sides.
- Combining distances on opposite sides without reading the diagram.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Draw horizontal ground and a vertical object, mark the right angle, then label the sightline and angle. Name the unknown before selecting a ratio. Keep units consistent.

### G. Quick revision summary
Translate the wording into a right triangle. Elevation/depression is relative to horizontal. Tangent links height and horizontal distance; sine/cosine involve the hypotenuse.

## Chapter 10: Circles

### A. Chapter overview
Tangents touch a circle at one point. Their relation to radii gives useful perpendicularity and equal-length results.

**Objectives:** state and prove the radius-tangent theorem; prove equal tangents from an external point; solve length problems.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
- A tangent meets a circle at exactly one point; that point is the point of contact.
- **Theorem:** The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point.
- Proof: if the tangent line were not perpendicular to radius $OP$, the perpendicular from centre $O$ to the line would have length less than $OP=r$. The foot would then be inside the circle, making the line intersect the circle at two points, contradicting tangency.
- **Equal tangents theorem:** tangents from an external point have equal lengths. Join the centre to both contact points; the two right triangles have equal hypotenuse (radii) and common hypotenuse from centre to external point, hence are congruent by RHS.

#### Visual guide: radius meets tangent at a right angle

```text
             tangent
        P────────T────────
          \      ⟂
           \     |
            \    | radius
             \   |
              \  O
```

The tangent touches the circle at $T$; radius $OT$ is perpendicular to the tangent there.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Theorems

| Theorem | Statement | Proof / conditions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Radius–tangent theorem | The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact | Proof required; if $PT$ is tangent at $T$, then $OT\perp PT$ |
| Equal tangents theorem | Tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal in length | Proof required; if $PA$ and $PB$ are tangents from $P$, then $PA=PB$ |

#### Useful relation

| Situation | Relationship | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Radius and tangent length | $r^2+t^2=OP^2$ | Right triangle formed by centre $O$, contact point, and external point $P$ |

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: Tangent length**

**Question:** A circle has radius 5 cm. An external point is 13 cm from the centre. Find the tangent length.

**Concept used:** Right triangle at point of contact.

**Solution:**
1. Radius is perpendicular to tangent, so $t^2+5^2=13^2$.
2. $t^2=144$, so $t=12$ (length is positive).

**Final answer:** **12 cm.**

**Exam tip:** Join the centre to the contact point before using Pythagoras.

**Example 2: Equal tangents**

**Question:** From point $P$, two tangents touch a circle at $A$ and $B$. If $PA=3x+1$ and $PB=5x-7$, find $x$ and tangent length.

**Concept used:** Equal tangents theorem.

**Solution:**
1. $3x+1=5x-7$.
2. $8=2x$, so $x=4$.
3. Each tangent is $3(4)+1=13$.

**Final answer:** **$x=4$; each tangent is 13 units.**

**Exam tip:** Equate lengths, then substitute back to report the length.

**Example 3: Perpendicular angle**

**Question:** A tangent touches a circle at $T$; $O$ is the centre. State $\angle OTP$ if $P$ lies on the tangent.

**Concept used:** Radius-tangent theorem.

**Solution:**
1. The radius $OT$ is perpendicular to the tangent at $T$.
2. Therefore the angle between $OT$ and $TP$ is a right angle.

**Final answer:** **$90^\circ$.**

**Exam tip:** The perpendicular relation is at the contact point.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Drawing a radius to an arbitrary point rather than the contact point.
- Applying equal tangents to segments that do not share an external endpoint.
- Taking a negative square root for a length.
- Claiming a tangent is perpendicular to a chord.
- Omitting the congruence reason in the equal-tangents proof.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Mark the contact points and right angles first. State the theorem used, then use Pythagoras or equal lengths. In proofs, list the matching sides and angles and name RHS.

### G. Quick revision summary
Radius to contact point is perpendicular to tangent. Tangents from the same external point are equal.

### H. Chapter-end revision checklist
- [ ] I can state and prove radius-tangent perpendicularity.
- [ ] I can prove equal tangent lengths using RHS.
- [ ] I can find tangent lengths with Pythagoras.
- [ ] I can identify the external point correctly.

### I. Practice questions
1. Radius 8 cm; centre-to-external-point distance 17 cm. Find tangent length.
2. Two tangent lengths from $P$ are $2x+9$ and $5x$. Find $x$ and their common length.
3. What angle does a radius make with the tangent at contact?

**Answers / hints:** 1. 15 cm. 2. $x=3$, length 15. 3. $90^\circ$.

## Chapter 11: Areas Related to Circles

### A. Chapter overview
Circle measures extend to sectors, arcs, segments, and composite shapes. A sector is a fraction of the full circle determined by its central angle.

**Objectives:** calculate circumference, area, arc length, sector area, segment area, and composite perimeter.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
A sector is bounded by two radii and an arc. A segment is bounded by a chord and its corresponding arc. Segment area is sector area minus the triangle formed by the radii and chord.

#### Visual guide: sector and segment

```text
              A
           .-'|`-.
         .'   |   `.
        /     |     \
       |      O──────B
        \   sector  /
         `-._____.-'
          chord AB
```

The sector is bounded by $OA$, $OB$, and the arc $AB$. The minor segment lies between chord $AB$ and that arc.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Formulae

| Quantity | Formula | Meaning and conditions | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Circumference | $2\pi r$ | $r$ is radius | Length around a full circle |
| Circle area | $\pi r^2$ | $r$ is radius | Region inside a full circle |
| Arc length | $\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}2\pi r$ | Central angle $\theta$ in degrees | Curved boundary of a sector |
| Sector area | $\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\pi r^2$ | Central angle $\theta$ in degrees | Region bounded by two radii and an arc |
| Segment area | Sector area $-$ triangle area | Use the matching chord and sector | Region between a chord and its arc |
| Sector perimeter | $2r+$ arc length | Includes both radii | Total boundary length of a sector |

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: Circumference and area**

**Question:** Find circumference and area for radius 7 cm, using $\pi=22/7$.

**Concept used:** Circle formulas.

**Solution:**
1. Circumference $=2(22/7)(7)=44$ cm.
2. Area $=(22/7)(49)=154$ cm$^2$.

**Final answer:** **44 cm; 154 cm$^2$.**

**Exam tip:** Circumference uses linear units; area uses square units.

**Example 2: Sector**

**Question:** Find the area and arc length of a $90^\circ$ sector of radius 14 cm, using $\pi=22/7$.

**Concept used:** Quarter-circle proportions.

**Solution:**
1. Area $=\frac14\pi(14)^2=154$ cm$^2$.
2. Arc length $=\frac14(2\pi\cdot14)=22$ cm.

**Final answer:** **Area 154 cm$^2$; arc 22 cm.**

**Exam tip:** Use the same angle fraction for arc and area.

**Example 3: Perimeter of a sector**

**Question:** Find the perimeter of a $60^\circ$ sector of radius 21 cm, using $\pi=22/7$.

**Concept used:** Two radii plus arc.

**Solution:**
1. Arc length $=(60/360)(2\pi\cdot21)=22$ cm.
2. Perimeter $=21+21+22=64$ cm.

**Final answer:** **64 cm.**

**Exam tip:** Sector perimeter includes both radii; arc length alone is not perimeter.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Using diameter where radius is required.
- Forgetting angle fraction $\theta/360^\circ$.
- Reporting area in linear units.
- Omitting radii from sector perimeter.
- Forgetting to subtract the triangle for a segment.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Identify whether the boundary includes straight radii or only curved arc. Mark the central angle and radius. Keep $\pi$ exact until the end unless a value is specified.

### G. Quick revision summary
Circle area is $\pi r^2$; circumference is $2\pi r$. Sector and arc are the corresponding fraction $\theta/360^\circ$ of the whole.

## Chapter 12: Surface Areas and Volumes

### A. Chapter overview
Solid geometry measures exposed surfaces and capacity. For combined solids, count only the surfaces visible on the outside.

**Objectives:** recall formulas; convert units; handle combinations of two prescribed solids and practical contexts.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
- Curved/lateral surface area excludes circular or polygonal ends; total surface area includes exposed faces.
- When two solids are joined along a face, that joined face is internal and is not included in the outside surface area.
- Convert all lengths to one unit before calculating. Volume units are cubic.

#### Visual guide: joined solids

```text
          _________
        /           \
       /  hemisphere \
      (_______________)
      |               |
      |    cylinder   |  exposed curved surfaces
      |               |
      |_______________|
       joined circular face is internal
```

Count only the outside boundary. The circular face where the hemisphere and cylinder meet is hidden inside the solid.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Solid formulae
Here, $r$ is radius, $h$ is perpendicular height, and $a$ is cube edge length. In the cuboid row, $l,b,h$ are its three edge lengths; for a cone, $l$ is slant height.

| Solid | Curved / lateral surface area | Total surface area | Volume |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cube, side $a$ | — | $6a^2$ | $a^3$ |
| Cuboid, $l,b,h$ | — | $2(lb+bh+hl)$ | $lbh$ |
| Right circular cylinder | $2\pi rh$ | $2\pi r(h+r)$ | $\pi r^2h$ |
| Right circular cone | $\pi rl$ | $\pi r(l+r)$ | $\frac13\pi r^2h$ |
| Sphere | $4\pi r^2$ | $4\pi r^2$ | $\frac43\pi r^3$ |
| Hemisphere | $2\pi r^2$ | $3\pi r^2$ | $\frac23\pi r^3$ |

For a right circular cone, $l=\sqrt{r^2+h^2}$. For a combination, add only exposed surfaces; do not count joined faces.

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: Cylinder volume**

**Question:** Find the volume of a cylinder of radius 3 cm and height 10 cm.

**Concept used:** Cylinder volume.

**Solution:**
1. $V=\pi r^2h$.
2. $V=\pi(3)^2(10)=90\pi$ cm$^3$.

**Final answer:** **$90\pi$ cm$^3$.**

**Exam tip:** Use perpendicular height in volume.

**Example 2: Cone surface area**

**Question:** A cone has radius 5 cm and height 12 cm. Find its curved surface area.

**Concept used:** Pythagoras and cone CSA.

**Solution:**
1. Slant height $l=\sqrt{5^2+12^2}=13$ cm.
2. CSA $=\pi rl=\pi(5)(13)=65\pi$ cm$^2$.

**Final answer:** **$65\pi$ cm$^2$.**

**Exam tip:** Do not substitute vertical height for slant height in CSA.

**Example 3: Joined cylinder and hemisphere**

**Question:** A hemisphere of radius 3 cm is attached to one end of a cylinder of the same radius and height 8 cm. Find the exposed surface area.

**Concept used:** Exposed surfaces only.

**Solution:**
1. Exposed area is cylinder curved area plus hemisphere curved area plus the cylinder's other circular end.
2. Area $=2\pi(3)(8)+2\pi(3^2)+\pi(3^2)$.
3. This is $48\pi+18\pi+9\pi=75\pi$ cm$^2$.

**Final answer:** **$75\pi$ cm$^2$.**

**Exam tip:** The joined circular face is internal, so exclude it.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Counting an internal joined face as exposed.
- Confusing cone height with slant height.
- Omitting the flat base of a hemisphere in total area.
- Mixing cm and m before calculation.
- Giving volume in square units or area in cubic units.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Sketch the solid and shade exposed surfaces. Write each component area separately, then add. For capacity, convert volume to the requested litre or millilitre unit after calculation.

### G. Quick revision summary
Use correct CSA/TSA/volume formula. Joined surfaces are not exposed. Check dimensions and units; area scales in square units, volume in cubic units.

### H. Chapter-end revision checklist
- [ ] I can distinguish CSA, TSA, and volume.
- [ ] I know the prescribed solid formulas.
- [ ] I can determine which surfaces are exposed.
- [ ] I can convert units before substituting.

### I. Practice questions
1. Find the volume of a sphere of radius 3 cm.
2. Find the total surface area of a hemisphere of radius 7 cm.
3. A cuboid measures $4\times3\times2$ cm. Find its volume.

**Answers / hints:** 1. $36\pi$ cm$^3$. 2. $462$ cm$^2$ using $\pi=22/7$. 3. 24 cm$^3$.

## Chapter 13: Statistics

### A. Chapter overview
Grouped data summarises many observations in intervals. Mean, median, and mode describe different aspects of its centre.

**Objectives:** find class marks; calculate mean by three methods; identify median/modal classes; calculate grouped median and mode; interpret results.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
- Class mark $x_i=(\text{lower limit}+\text{upper limit})/2$.
- Direct mean uses $\sum f_ix_i/\sum f_i$.
- Assumed-mean method sets $d_i=x_i-a$; step-deviation sets $u_i=(x_i-a)/h$.
- Median class contains the $N/2$th observation in cumulative frequencies.
- Modal class has the highest frequency.

#### Visual guide: grouped frequencies

```text
Frequency
  8 |       ████████
  6 |       ████████  ██████
  4 | ████  ████████  ██████
  2 | ████  ████████  ██████  ████
    +----------------------------→ Class interval
       0–10  10–20    20–30   30–40
```

The tallest bar identifies the modal class; cumulative frequencies are used to locate the median class.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Formulae

| Measure / method | Formula | Meaning and conditions | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Class mark | $x_i=\frac{\text{lower limit}+\text{upper limit}}2$ | Midpoint of class $i$ | Represent a class by one value |
| Direct mean | $\bar{x}=\frac{\sum f_ix_i}{N}$ | $N=\sum f_i$; $x_i$ class mark | Find the frequency-weighted average |
| Assumed-mean mean | $\bar{x}=a+\frac{\sum f_id_i}{N}$ | $d_i=x_i-a$; $a$ is assumed mean | Simplify calculations using deviations |
| Step-deviation mean | $\bar{x}=a+h\frac{\sum f_iu_i}{N}$ | $u_i=(x_i-a)/h$; $h$ is common class width | Simplify further when class widths are equal |
| Grouped median | $l+\frac{(N/2-cf)}{f}h$ | $l$ lower boundary of median class; $cf$ cumulative frequency before it; $f$ its frequency; $h$ class width | Estimate the middle value of grouped observations |
| Grouped mode | $l+\frac{f_1-f_0}{2f_1-f_0-f_2}h$ | $l$ lower boundary of modal class; $f_1$ modal frequency; $f_0,f_2$ adjacent frequencies; $h$ class width | Estimate the most frequent value |

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: Direct mean**

**Question:** Values $x_i$ are 10, 20; frequencies are 2, 3. Find the mean.

**Concept used:** Direct method.

**Solution:**
1. $\sum f_ix_i=2(10)+3(20)=80$.
2. $N=2+3=5$.
3. Mean $=80/5=16$.

**Final answer:** **16.**

**Exam tip:** The total frequency is the denominator.

**Example 2: Median**

**Question:** Classes 0–10, 10–20, 20–30 have frequencies 4, 8, 8. Find the grouped median, using continuous class boundaries.

**Concept used:** Median formula.

**Solution:**
1. $N=20$, so $N/2=10$. Cumulative frequencies are 4, 12, 20; median class is 10–20.
2. $l=10,cf=4,f=8,h=10$.
3. Median $=10+\frac{10-4}{8}(10)=17.5$.

**Final answer:** **17.5.**

**Exam tip:** $cf$ means cumulative frequency before the median class.

**Example 3: Mode**

**Question:** For classes 0–10, 10–20, 20–30, frequencies 3, 8, 5, estimate the mode.

**Concept used:** Grouped mode.

**Solution:**
1. Modal class is 10–20. Thus $l=10,h=10,f_1=8,f_0=3,f_2=5$.
2. Mode $=10+\frac{8-3}{16-3-5}(10)=10+\frac{50}{8}=16.25$.

**Final answer:** **16.25.**

**Exam tip:** Identify the frequencies before, in, and after the modal class.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Using class limits instead of class marks for mean.
- Miscalculating cumulative frequencies.
- Selecting median class by highest frequency rather than $N/2$.
- Using the wrong adjacent frequencies in the mode formula.
- Omitting class width or using inconsistent boundaries.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Make a table with class, class mark, frequency, and required products. For median/mode, record all formula symbols beside the selected class. Interpret the result in the data's units and context.

### G. Quick revision summary
Mean is frequency-weighted average. Median locates the middle via cumulative frequency. Mode estimates the most frequent value using the modal class and its neighbours.

### H. Chapter-end revision checklist
- [ ] I can compute class marks and total frequency.
- [ ] I can use direct, assumed-mean, and step-deviation means.
- [ ] I can locate the median and modal classes.
- [ ] I can substitute grouped median/mode values correctly.

### I. Practice questions
1. Find the mean for $x_i=5,10,15$ and $f_i=1,2,1$.
2. Frequencies are 2, 6, 2 in consecutive classes 0–10, 10–20, 20–30. Identify the median class.
3. For modal-class frequency 12, preceding 7, following 5, class width 10 and lower boundary 20, find mode.

**Answers / hints:** 1. 10. 2. 10–20. 3. 25.

## Chapter 14: Probability

### A. Chapter overview
Classical probability compares favourable equally likely outcomes with all possible outcomes.

**Objectives:** define experiments, outcomes, and events; calculate simple probabilities; use complements; check bounds.

### B. Important concepts and explanations
- A random experiment has an uncertain outcome from a known set of possibilities.
- An event is a set of outcomes. In the classical model, outcomes must be equally likely.
- Probability is between 0 and 1 inclusive. An impossible event has probability 0; a certain event has probability 1.

#### Visual guide: outcomes of a coin toss

```text
                 Start
                /     \
           Heads       Tails
            1/2          1/2
```

For a fair coin, the two outcomes are equally likely; an event may contain one or both outcomes.

### C. Formula and theorem sheet

#### Formulae and properties

| Name | Formula / property | Meaning and conditions | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Classical probability | $P(E)=\frac{n(E)}{n(S)}$ | Finite sample space $S$ with equally likely outcomes; $n(E)$ counts favourable outcomes | Find an event's probability by counting |
| Complement | $P(E')=1-P(E)$ | $E'$ means event $E$ does not occur | Find the probability of “not $E$” or simplify “at least one” |
| Probability bounds | $0\le P(E)\le1$ | Applies to every event | Check whether a calculated probability is possible |

### D. Solved board-style examples
**Example 1: Die**

**Question:** A fair die is rolled. Find the probability of getting a multiple of 3.

**Concept used:** Equally likely outcomes.

**Solution:**
1. Outcomes are 1 to 6; favourable outcomes are 3 and 6.
2. Probability $=2/6=1/3$.

**Final answer:** **$1/3$.**

**Exam tip:** List the sample space to avoid missing outcomes.

**Example 2: Complement**

**Question:** A fair coin is tossed three times. Find the probability of at least one head.

**Concept used:** Complement.

**Solution:**
1. There are $2^3=8$ equally likely sequences.
2. The complement is no heads, namely TTT, with probability $1/8$.
3. Required probability $=1-1/8=7/8$.

**Final answer:** **$7/8$.**

**Exam tip:** “At least one” is often simpler via its complement.

**Example 3: Numbered objects**

**Question:** One card is selected at random from cards numbered 1 to 10. Find the probability of selecting a prime number.

**Concept used:** Count favourable outcomes.

**Solution:**
1. Primes are 2, 3, 5, 7: four favourable outcomes.
2. Total outcomes are 10.
3. Probability $=4/10=2/5$.

**Final answer:** **$2/5$.**

**Exam tip:** 1 is not prime.

### E. Common examination mistakes
- Assuming outcomes are equally likely when they are not.
- Counting 1 as a prime.
- Using favourable outcomes as denominator.
- Forgetting to reduce a fraction.
- Reporting a probability outside $[0,1]$.

### F. Exam tips and problem-solving strategy
Write the sample space or count total outcomes systematically. Identify the event precisely, count favourable outcomes, simplify, then check the probability bounds. Use complements for “at least one” events.

### G. Quick revision summary
For finite equally likely outcomes, probability is favourable over total. Complement probability is $1-P(E)$. Every probability is between 0 and 1 inclusive.

### H. Chapter-end revision checklist
- [ ] I can describe an experiment, outcome, and event.
- [ ] I can count equally likely outcomes.
- [ ] I can calculate complements.
- [ ] I can verify the probability lies in $[0,1]$.

### I. Practice questions
1. Roll a fair die. Find the probability of a number greater than 4.
2. Draw one card from a standard 52-card deck. Find the probability it is a heart.
3. A bag has 3 red and 5 blue balls. Find the probability of not drawing red.

**Answers / hints:** 1. $1/3$. 2. $1/4$. 3. $5/8$.
